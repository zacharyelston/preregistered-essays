# Answerability, Regularity, and the Social Fact

Many facts about social life are, at first glance, regularities. People in a city turn right at most intersections. Colleagues send status emails at certain hours. A friend has coffee at seven. When one of these patterns breaks, we do not always react in the same way. If a colleague skips a queue, we may ask for an excuse, blame him, or say he has acted impolitely. If my friend does not have coffee at seven, we are at most surprised and revise a forecast. This contrast is not trivial. It separates facts that are genuinely social—facts of standing, practice, and institution—from mere coincidental regularities in behavior.

I will argue that a genuine social fact constitutes a shared space of normative answerability. Within such a space, deviation from the relevant regularity is treated as a potential violation. It may be excused, justified, or sanctioned. A coincidental regularity, by contrast, yields only failed expectations. The point is not that every institution enforces its standards, or that every norm is accompanied by punishment. Rather, the distinctive mark of a social fact is that it makes answerability possible: agents can be held, and can hold themselves, accountable for failing to conform.

This claim is likely to appear circular. The vocabulary of violation, excuse, and sanction is itself social. To say that queueing is normative may be to assume that one has already recognized queueing as an institution. I will answer that objection by spelling out a non-question-begging test. The test does not look for a non-social marker; no such marker could exist. It looks for a distinctive pattern of second-person reason-giving that can be observed before the classification is made.

## 1. Normative answerability

Answerability is not mere causal responsibility. A person may cause a window to break by throwing a ball, but if no social standard is at issue, no one demands an account in the relevant sense. In the present sense, an agent is answerable to others for an action relative to a standard when the others may reasonably demand an account, and the account can be accepted or rejected on grounds that bear on whether the action was permissible, whether the agent was at fault, and what follows for the relationship. The demand is second-personal: it addresses the agent as a participant who had a reason to act otherwise and who can understand the reason. It is not a third-personal request for a causal explanation, though it may involve one.

The standard need not be explicit. A queueing practice may be so familiar that nobody states it. But the participants must be able to discriminate among cases: an emergency may excuse, ignorance may excuse, deliberate self-interest may not. The practice must have a structure of exceptions. Without that structure, there is no violation, only an outlier. The shared space is therefore not a set of beliefs about what usually happens; it is a framework for assigning and challenging standings. To be in the queue is to have a standing relative to those behind you. To cut the queue is to threaten that standing. The fact that the queue exists is not merely the fact that bodies happen to be arranged in a line; it is the fact that the arrangement is recognized as creating permissions and obligations.

This is why answerability is normative in a minimal sense. It involves reasons. A reason is a consideration that can count in favor of or against an action (Scanlon, 1998). A standard becomes normatively significant for a group when the group treats conformance as a reason for action and nonconformance as a reason for criticism. The group need not think the standard is morally correct. A club rule may be arbitrary. A legal rule may be unjust. The normativity is internal to the practice: the practice supplies reasons that participants recognize as applicable, even if they also recognize that the practice can be revised.

The shared character of the space matters. If I privately expect my neighbor to mow his lawn on Saturday, his failure may frustrate me, but he is not answerable to me in the relevant sense unless there is a shared standard under which his failure is a breach. The shared standard need not be the object of an explicit common intention; it may be a background practice. But it must be mutually recognized in at least a minimal way: the demand for an account is understood as appropriate, and the account is understood as capable of answering the demand.

It is also important that answerability is not identical to normativity in general. There are epistemic, moral, and prudential norms that do not by themselves constitute social facts. What makes a norm social is that it organizes a second-person space in which agents can hold one another responsible. A norm of rationality may tell an individual that she has a reason not to believe a contradiction, but if no one can address her as a participant in a shared practice of belief, the norm does not create a social fact in the present sense. The social fact is created when the norm is embedded in mutual accountability.

## 2. Violations and failed predictions

A violation is not any deviation. It is a deviation that matters because it is a breach of a standard that the agent had a reason to meet. A failed prediction is a deviation from a statistical regularity. The difference is not that one reaction is emotional and the other calm. Both can be accompanied by regret, surprise, or annoyance. The difference is in the form of the social relation.

When a prediction fails, the natural response is to update the model. If my friend has had coffee at seven for months and one day has not, I may think: perhaps he is out of coffee, perhaps he forgot, perhaps he is ill. My expectation is revised. I may worry, but I do not normally demand that he answer for the absence. If he offers an explanation, it is an explanation of a change in his behavior, not a justification of a wrong. The explanation is relevant to my future forecast, not to his standing.

When a norm is breached, the natural response is to demand an account that bears on permission and fault. If a person skips a queue, the response may be: “Why are you doing that?” This question is not merely a request for a cause. It is a request for a reason that could make the action permissible: an emergency, a mistake, an exemption, a challenge to the queue itself. The answer can be accepted or rejected. If accepted, the apparent violation is reclassified as an excused or justified deviation. If rejected, the violation stands, and the relationship may be altered through blame, apology, exclusion, or sanction.

The same sentence can function differently in the two cases. “I was out of town” may explain why my friend did not have coffee at seven. It may also justify why I did not bring coffee to a shared meeting. In the first case, it changes the predictor’s model. In the second, it changes the moral or social assessment of the agent. The difference is not in the content of the sentence but in the social structure to which it is offered. In the first structure, the agent is a variable in a pattern; in the second, the agent is a participant in a space of reasons.

This is why the availability of exceptions is a mark of normativity. A mere regularity has no exceptions in the relevant sense. It has outliers, new causes, and changed conditions. A norm has a practice of distinguishing cases in which the standard applies from cases in which it does not. The queueing norm may permit an emergency; the promise to bring coffee may be discharged by impossibility. The practice of exceptions is not optional. Without it, a standard would be a bare command with no social meaning. A genuine norm is a standard that can be interpreted, criticized, and applied to particular cases. Even a rigid norm must allow some distinction between violations and non-violations, such as accidents, coercion, or impossibility; otherwise there is no answerability, only an automatic response to behavior.

## 3. Institutional facts and the common thread of answerability

This account fits the broader problem in social ontology. Searle’s institutional facts are brute facts plus a status function: a piece of paper counts as money, a person counts as a president, a line of people counts as a queue (Searle, 1995). But a status function is not a mere label. It creates a position that carries rights, permissions, and obligations. To be a president is to be able to make decisions with authority; to be money is to be a recognized medium of exchange. These positions generate answerability. If an official acts improperly, the question “What do you mean by that?” is appropriate. If a payment is refused, the question “Why are you not accepting legal tender?” may be appropriate. The institutional fact is not only that the status exists; it is that the status places the holder in a field of reason-giving.

Hart’s distinction between habits and rules points in the same direction. A rule creates an obligation because it gives rise to criticism and to an internal point of view: participants treat the rule as a standard for conduct and for criticism (Hart, 1961). Strawson’s reactive attitudes—resentment, indignation, gratitude, forgiveness—are the grammar of answerability. They are appropriate only when the agent is understood as capable of understanding reasons and responding to them (Strawson, 1958). Bratman’s account of common intention shows how shared undertakings create mutual accountability: participants can criticize one another for failing to contribute to the joint project (Bratman, 1992).

None of these theories reduces social facts to behavior. They all recognize that social facts involve a space in which agents can address one another as responsible participants. The answerability thesis captures the minimal common thread. A fact is genuinely social, in the strong sense that matters for institutions, when it is constituted by such a space. A mere regularity, however stable, is not. It may be a social regularity in a weak sense—a pattern in the behavior of a group—but it is not an institutional fact if it confers no standing and creates no answerability.

This also explains why Lewis’s conventions are not merely regularities, although they can look like them (Lewis, 1969). A convention is a regularity of interdependent expectations, but it is not just a statistical fact that people usually do X. It is a regularity that is recognized as a solution to a coordination problem and as a standard against which deviations can be assessed. If everyone usually turns right at an intersection, and one driver turns left, the driver may be criticized not because the turning pattern is a law of nature, but because the pattern is a shared standard.

A regularity can, of course, be a prudential reason to conform without being a norm in the present sense. If everyone in a building speaks quietly, I may have a reason not to shout. But if the quietness is only a coincidental regularity, my shouting may be foolish or annoying without being a violation. The difference is whether the quietness has been taken up as a standard for conduct, such that shouting would warrant a reason-demand and a change in standing. The answerability test separates mere coordination from social normativity.

## 4. The circularity objection

The strongest objection to this thesis is that it is question-begging. The criterion treats the availability of a vocabulary of violation, exception, and sanction as the mark of a genuine social fact. But those attitudes are themselves social facts. To say that skipping a queue is answerable while not having coffee at seven is merely a failed prediction appears to assume that queueing is already recognized as an institution. The argument then seems to supply no independent test for when a regularity has become normative. It simply says: when the relevant social attitudes are present. But those attitudes are precisely what are supposed to be explained.

The objection is fair if the criterion is read as a verbal one. If all that were required were that people use words like “violation” or “norm,” the argument would indeed be circular. One could label a random regularity with normative language and thereby declare it institutional. But the criterion I am defending is not lexical. It is functional and structural. It concerns the role that a regularity plays in a shared practice of reason-giving. That role can be identified without already knowing whether the case is an institution.

## 5. A non-question-begging test

First, the demand for independence must be understood correctly. A test for a social fact cannot be wholly non-social. Social facts are not physical objects with a non-social signature. If the question is when a regularity has become normative, the answer must be given in terms of social relations: reasons, standings, and second-person addresses. The relevant independence is not independence from social life, but independence from the conclusion in a particular case. We must be able to apply the test to a candidate regularity before deciding whether it is a genuine social fact. The test I offer meets that requirement.

The test has two layers. The first layer is the regularity itself: a pattern of actions by members of a group in relevant contexts. The second layer is the pattern of social responses to deviations from that regularity. A regularity R is normative for a group G iff the second layer has the following structure:

1. G’s members treat R as a standard for conduct, not merely as a description of what usually happens. This treatment can be tacit.
2. When an individual deviates from R, others in G treat the deviation as warranting a second-person reason-demand: a request for an account that bears on whether the deviation was permissible.
3. The reason-demand admits a range of answers that can alter the social assessment of the deviant: excuses, justifications, admissions, challenges to the standard, or appeals to exceptions.
4. The outcome of the account is tied to a change in standing: blame, apology, trust revision, sanction, or restored standing.
5. The regularity is maintained, at least in part, by these corrective practices.

These conditions do not use the concept of an institution. They describe observable social features. They can be applied to a candidate regularity. We ask what happens when the regularity is broken. Do people merely revise their forecasts? Or do they demand an account? If an account is demanded, does it have the force of an answer? Can it be accepted or rejected? Does its acceptance change the relationship? If yes, the regularity is normative. If no, it is a mere regularity.

The first condition may appear to smuggle in normativity: to treat something as a standard is to treat it normatively. But “standard” here is not the conclusion. It is a more basic relational concept. A standard is a criterion used to assess actions. We can identify that criterion by the practice of assessment itself. If a group consistently distinguishes cases of conformance from cases of nonconformance, and if the distinction affects their responses, there is a standard in the relevant sense. The test does not require us to infer normativity from the word “standard”; it requires us to observe the structure of assessment.

The second condition is the core. A reason-demand is a distinctive speech act. It is not a question about causes in general. “Why did you skip the queue?” is not equivalent to “What caused you to skip the queue?” The first question asks for a reason that could make the action permissible or explain why the standard did not apply. The second asks for a causal story that may be irrelevant to fault. Austin’s analysis of speech acts helps here: a demand for a reason has a distinctive illocutionary force. It is a demand that the speaker supply a consideration that can bear on permission (Austin, 1962). If no such consideration is available, the demand is not defeated by a causal explanation. “I was in a hurry” may be a cause; whether it is an excuse depends on the standard. An isolated reason-demand is not, by itself, a sufficient marker of a normative social space. It may be a one-off rebuke, a failed attempt to create a standard, or a misclassification of a situation. Its diagnostic value is reliable only when it occurs within a recurrent, mutually recognized pattern in which such demands are understood as appropriate, accounts can be assessed, and outcomes alter standing.

The third condition is what separates norms from rigid regularities. A mere pattern can have statistical exceptions, but it lacks a practice of exceptions. In a norm, a deviation can be reclassified. The person who skips the queue may say: “I did not see you,” or “I have a medical emergency.” The group can assess whether the explanation fits an exception. In a mere regularity, the same sentence is only an explanation of a change. If my friend does not have coffee at seven and says “I am out of coffee,” the sentence explains the absence. It does not answer a demand that he should have had coffee. The difference is that the first case has a background practice for deciding when the standard is suspended; the second does not.

The fourth condition ties the account to standing. Answerability is not merely linguistic. It is social because the outcome of reason-giving changes the relationship. If a violation is established, the deviant may be blamed, punished, excluded, or required to apologize. If an excuse is accepted, standing may be restored. If no such change is possible, there is no answerability. This does not mean that sanctions must occur. A norm may be maintained by internalized feelings of shame or by the mere possibility of disapproval. What matters is that the practice makes sanction possible: the participants can conceive of a deviation as something for which an agent can be held responsible.

The fifth condition is important for distinguishing stable habits from institutions. If a regularity is maintained only by individual preferences or physical constraints, and deviations are never addressed as answerable, the regularity is not institutional. If the regularity is maintained partly by corrections—by people calling out deviations, by apologies, by teaching, by sanction—then the second-order practices are part of what keeps the first-order regularity in place. This is not a circular explanation. It is an explanation of the transition: a regularity becomes normative when it is taken up in a practice of reason-giving. The regularity is no longer merely a description of what people do; it becomes a reason for what they should do and a standard for evaluating what they did.

The objection may reply that the test still uses normative concepts such as “permissible,” “fault,” and “standing.” But this is not a vice; it is a consequence of the subject matter. The question is when a regularity has become normative, so the answer must involve normativity. The issue is whether the test presupposes the conclusion in the particular case. It does not. The concepts used in the test are more basic than the concept of an institution. A reason-demand, an excuse, and a change of standing can be identified in social interaction without knowing whether the underlying regularity is an institution. The test is a diagnostic, not a stipulation.

A concrete case shows the point. Suppose we observe a group of people standing in a line outside a theater. One person pushes to the front. If the others merely say, “He is unusual,” or “The line is not very reliable,” and then adjust their expectations, the regularity may be only a habit. But if they say, “You cannot do that,” and the person says, “I am sorry, I did not see the queue,” and they accept the apology and reestablish the line, the test is satisfied. We do not need to know beforehand that queueing is an institution. We observe a second-person demand, an offered account, an assessment of the account, and a change in the social relationship. The normative character of the practice is revealed by the structure of the interaction, not assumed by the label.

Conversely, suppose a person has coffee at seven every morning for a year. One day she has none. Her colleague says, “Oh, she must be out of coffee.” No demand for an account is made. No one feels entitled to blame her. The regularity has generated an expectation, but not a standard. The absence is a failed prediction. It changes the colleague’s model of her behavior. It does not change her standing. The test therefore classifies the regularity as non-normative, at least with respect to the colleague. If, however, the colleague and the person have a shared ritual in which coffee at seven is a promise, and the absence prompts the question “Why didn’t you bring coffee?”, the same behavior is now situated in a space of answerability.

The objection also asks for an independent test for when a regularity has become normative. The answer is that the transition is marked by the appearance of second-order reason-giving. There may be no sharp event. A practice may move gradually from habit to norm. But the conceptual test remains stable. At the initial stage, deviations are treated as changes in a pattern. At the later stage, deviations are treated as breaches that require an account. The transition is not a physical change in the frequency of behavior; it is a change in the form of the social responses to behavior. This is exactly the kind of fact that social ontology needs to identify, because it is the fact that makes the regularity more than a regularity.

Finally, the fact that the test is social does not make it question-begging. The referee is right that the attitudes of blame, excuse, and sanction are social facts. But they are not the same social fact as the institution in question. They are lower-level features of the interaction that constitute the higher-level fact. To use the presence of a reason-demand to identify a norm is not to assume that the norm exists; it is to identify the relational structure that makes the norm possible. The analogy is with promises. We do not identify a promise by a non-linguistic physical fact that exists independently of the utterance. We identify it by the social structure of offer, acceptance, and expectation of performance. That structure is itself part of what a promise is. It is not circular to use the structure as a criterion, provided the structure is specified independently of the particular case.

The test also avoids another potential circularity. It does not require that participants articulate a complete institutional theory. They need not be able to explain why the practice exists, what its historical origin is, or what abstract principle justifies it. They need only display the local competences that make answerability possible: recognizing a deviation, demanding a reason, assessing the reason, and altering standing accordingly. A community with a rich normative life may have many norms it cannot fully articulate. The test remains applicable because it tracks public practice rather than explicit doctrine.

It is also important that the test does not require unanimity. A shared space is not a space in which everyone agrees on every case. Norms are often contested, criticized, and revised. What is required is that the practice has a common framework within which such disputes occur. Participants may disagree about whether an excuse is good, but they can recognize that the question has the form of a question about an excuse rather than a question about a prediction. The shared space is constituted by the possibility of common recognition, not by actual agreement on every detail.

## 6. When a regularity becomes normative

The criterion also clarifies the developmental question. A regularity becomes normative when it is taken up by a community as a standard for conduct and evaluation. This can happen by explicit rule-making, by gradual mutual expectation, or by a crisis that reveals the social meaning of the practice. In each case, the mark is not an increase in frequency but a change in the type of response to deviation. People begin to treat the regularity as something for which agents can be held accountable. They begin to distinguish violations from exceptions. They begin to use reason-giving to maintain the regularity.

This may be a matter of degree. Some practices are strongly normative; others are weak. A fashion trend may generate ridicule, which is a sanction, but the trend may be too unstable to create strong standing. A traffic convention may generate strong answerability because of legal sanctions. A shared mealtime may generate mild answerability because missing it is treated as a slight. The distinction is not between norm and non-norm as a sharp physical boundary; it is between a space of answerability and no such space. The test can locate the point at which a regularity crosses from the latter to the former, even if the strength of the norm varies.

The thesis, then, is not that institutions are social facts because people describe them with normative language. It is that the normative language is not a mere description of an independently existing institution; it is the medium in which the institution is constituted. A genuine social fact creates a shared space in which agents can be asked to answer for their deviations. A coincidental regularity does not. It yields failed expectations, not violations.

The circularity objection is answered by the reason-demand test. The test is social, as it must be. It is not question-begging, because it specifies an observable structure of second-person reason-giving that can be applied before the classification is made. A regularity is normative when deviations from it are treated as warranting an account, when the account can alter the agent’s standing, and when the regularity is maintained by these corrective practices. This gives social ontology a precise criterion for distinguishing institutional facts from mere social regularities. The mark of a genuine social fact is not sanction, not belief, not frequency, but answerability.
