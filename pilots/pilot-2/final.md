# Promising From an Office: Succession, Seats, and the Reach of a Promise

## 1. The thesis and the challenge

A promise reaches forward in time. What sets how far it reaches? The usual answer is personal identity. The person who promised is the person who must perform, and the promise lapses when that person ceases to exist. On this answer, wherever the later bearer of a promise is not psychologically continuous with the promisor, as in severe dementia, total amnesia, or a reset and redeployed AI system, there is simply no promisor left to be bound.

I defend a different answer. Every promise is issued from a *position*. The promise's temporal and personal reach is fixed by that position's *succession rules*: the norms that settle which later occupant, if any, holds the place the promise was made from. Some positions are offices in the familiar sense: corporations, trusteeships, estates, the deployed role of "the assistant on this account." Natural persons promise from a position too, the position of being this person. That position has a default succession rule, and psychological continuity does most of the work in it. Identity seems essential to promising because this default operates in nearly every case and nobody has to state it. It is not mandatory. Alternative succession rules can be set up, within limits I specify, and where none applies the promise lapses.

A referee has raised a serious objection, and answering it forces the view into its correct form. The objection runs as follows. Office-holders who inherit commitments are bound either because they *accepted* the office or because a *legitimate authority* imposed role-duties on them. Neither source is the original promise. The corporate case does not even involve succession of obligors, since the corporation persists as a group agent through its decision procedures (List and Pettit 2011). Employees carry duties they agreed to, which are duties to discharge the corporation's obligations. For natural persons, nobody enacts the "default succession rule," so its authority is unexplained. In the cases the thesis targets, the later stage has accepted nothing. If the earlier stage sets the succession rule, it binds a distinct agent without consent, which violates the principle that one cannot promise on another's behalf without authority (Hart 1955; Raz 1972; Owens 2012). If carers, deployers or the law set the rule, the result is imposed role-responsibility, and the subject has changed from promising to stipulation.

My reply has four parts.

1. The objection relies on a sharp dichotomy between "the same obligor persists" and "a distinct agent is bound through succession." The corporate case shows that this dichotomy is not drawn prior to succession rules. Sameness of obligor across time is *constituted* by succession rules (§2).
2. "Being bound" by a promise has two components: being the *seat* of the obligation and being an *executor* of it. Acceptance and imposed authority govern executorship. Seat-succession is governed by the promise together with the office's rules, and a test based on release shows that the resulting obligation is promissory, not stipulated (§3).
3. The demand for an independent authority behind the default rule proves too much. It applies equally to ordinary intrapersonal promising. Psychological continuity cannot be the whole default anyway, because it cannot count obligors under branching (§4).
4. The earlier stage's power to fix non-default succession rules is a normative power justified in the usual Razian way. It is not assumed, and it is bounded by four independently motivated limits. Those limits respect, and also explain, the principle against promising on another's behalf (§5).

I then revisit the three cases (§6) and residual worries (§7). The core thesis survives, more precisely stated: a non-continuous later stage is bound only if the promise was made from an office whose succession rules select it, and identity matters only as the default such rule.

## 2. The corporate case: what the objection concedes

Begin with the referee's strongest factual point: in the corporate case the promisor never changes, since the corporation is the obligor throughout. I accept this but deny that it disposes of the generalization.

Ask what makes the corporation the *same* obligor after complete staff turnover. List and Pettit's answer is that group agents persist through their organizational structure. The corporation is the same agent because its decision procedures, which settle how members' inputs become group attitudes and actions, continue to operate. Members can come and go. But "the decision procedures continue to operate" is not a natural fact like the persistence of a lump of bronze. It holds because there are rules that say who counts as a director, how directors are replaced, what happens on merger or acquisition, and when the entity is dissolved and its obligations transferred to a liquidator. Take those rules away and the question "is this the same corporation?" has no answer. A firm that sells all its assets to a new firm with the same staff, building and name may or may not be the obligor its predecessor was. The answer depends on whether the transaction was structured as a merger, an asset sale, or a successor-liability arrangement. Psychological or functional continuity on the ground does not settle it. The rules do.

So the corporate case does not show a promise passing from one obligor to a distinct one. It shows something more useful: *the identity of an obligor over time can be fixed by succession rules rather than by any form of psychological continuity.* The referee's description, "a persisting group agent's promise is carried out by role-holders," is accurate. But the persistence it mentions is an output of succession rules. It is not an independent input that makes them redundant.

The objection's dilemma depends on a contrast. Either the later agent is the same obligor, in which case no succession is needed, or it is a distinct agent, in which case authority is needed to bind it. That contrast takes it for granted that *metaphysics* settles sameness of obligor before the normative rules come in. For corporations that is plainly false, as the objector's own source shows. My generalizing claim is that it is false for natural persons as well. "Same obligor" is a normative status, the status of occupying the same office. Its relation to metaphysical personal identity is contingent, though in the default case the two are close.

Hobbes had the relevant concept. He defined a person as one "whose words or actions are considered either as his own, or as representing the words or actions of an other man," and he called this a matter of "personation" (Hobbes 1651, ch. XVI). Locke, writing on identity, called "person" a "forensic term, appropriating actions and their merit" (Locke 1694, II.xxvii.26). Both treated the person to whom commitments attach as a normative position as well as a metaphysical kind. My thesis is that, for promising, the positional sense governs and the metaphysical sense contributes the default.

This reshapes the problem without yet answering the objection. The referee can still ask what binds the *later stage*, the human being with dementia or the redeployed model, when that stage has accepted nothing. To answer, we need to see that "binding the later stage" covers two different relations.

## 3. Seats and executors

### 3.1 The distinction

Consider an obligation that plainly survives the end of psychological continuity: a debt owed by someone who has died. The debtor no longer exists in any sense. The debt is not extinguished. It is a claim against the *estate*, which an executor or administrator settles before anything passes to the heirs. Heirs are not personally liable and may disclaim their inheritance. The executor has duties because they accepted the office, or because a court appointed them after nobody else would. The obligation itself, meaning the creditor's claim, is what the deceased created. The executor's acceptance does not create it.

The same holds in the moral case. Suppose you promised your nephew, "the clock will be yours when I die." After your death we think your estate owes him the clock, and an executor who sold it to a dealer would wrong him: the wrong would be a failure to honor *your promise*, not a failure in some separate duty of the executor's own making. The nephew's complaint concerns what *he was promised*.

This case contains two relations that the ordinary intrapersonal case runs together.

- **The seat** of an obligation is the position against which the promisee's claim stands. Its holdings, arrangements and future conduct are what the promise constrains. If the promise is broken, the breach is attributed to the seat, and repair is owed from it.
- **An executor** is an agent who has a duty to bring about the conduct or outcome the promise requires, acting in the seat's name or for its sake.

When I promise to meet you for lunch, I am the seat and the sole executor, and the two coincide so completely that nobody separates them. The corporation is the seat, and its officers are executors. The estate is the seat, and the administrator is the executor.

### 3.2 What the dilemma actually targets

Now reread the dilemma. It asks where *the successor's* obligation comes from and finds two sources: acceptance and imposed authority. Both are good accounts of *executor* duties. The employee's duty to deliver what the corporation promised comes from her employment terms. The administrator's duty comes from accepting the office or from court appointment. The proxy's duty to carry out an incapacitated person's commitments comes from accepting proxyship. I grant all of this. The office view does not say the original promise directly creates executor duties in strangers.

The thesis concerns the seat. It says that a non-continuous later stage is bound by an earlier promise only if, under the office's succession rules, it occupies the seat the promise was made from. The objection says nothing to show that seat-succession needs the later stage's acceptance or an external authority's imposition. The estate case shows it needs neither. A claim against an estate exists even when no executor has been appointed and nobody wants the job. The promisee's claim does not wait on anyone accepting anything. Executor acceptance makes a difference to *who must act*. It makes no difference to *whether there is something owed*.

### 3.3 The release test: promising, not stipulation

The referee's sharpest charge is that, if carers, deployers or the law set the rule, "the thesis has changed the subject from promising to stipulated responsibility." This charge can be tested, since an obligation's source is shown by who can alter it. The test has four parts.

1. **Release.** Whose release extinguishes the obligation? In the corporate case, if the CEO is dismissed, the corporation's promise to its supplier still stands, and a new executor must discharge it. If the supplier releases the corporation, every executor's duty to discharge it disappears at once. The employer can release an executor from her role. It cannot release the promise. Only the promisee can do that.
2. **Content.** What fixes what is owed? The terms of the promise do. The terms of the executor's role do not. An administrator's general duty is "settle the estate's debts"; *which* debts and *what* they require is determined by the deceased's commitments.
3. **Direction.** To whom is the wrong of non-performance done? It is done to the promisee, and her grievance has promissory form: *I was promised this.* A creditor's complaint against an estate is not that the administrator failed his employer.
4. **Persistence through executor change.** Does the obligation survive when the executor changes? It does. That is exactly what we saw in the corporate case.

An obligation that meets all four conditions is promissory in every respect that matters. The promisee's normative power to waive it, which Owens (2012) puts at the center of promissory obligation, belongs to the promisee alone. Role-obligations imposed by a third party fail the test, since the imposing authority can release them and fixes their content. In the cases the thesis is built for, the obligation meets the test, and the role-duties of executors are *duties to discharge an existing promissory obligation*. Their content and direction come from that obligation. So the subject has not changed. The promise is still the thing being discharged, and executor duties are the means by which a seat that cannot act for itself, or cannot act alone, discharges it.

### 3.4 What it means for a later stage to be "bound"

This lets me state the thesis exactly. A later stage *L* is bound by a promise made by an earlier stage *E* when:

- (a) *L* occupies the seat *E* promised from, under the office's succession rules; and
- (b) to the extent that *L* is able to act on the promise, and the promise's content may be performed by *L*, *L* is a default executor.

Clause (a) is the core of the thesis and covers severe dementia. Clause (b) covers cases where the later stage is an agent. A person with severe dementia may be the seat, so that her care, her home and her money are what the promise constrains, without being an executor, because she cannot deliberate about the promise at all. This is not a special exemption. It is how minors' estates, insolvent firms and estates of the dead are treated: they owe without acting.

## 4. The authority of the default

### 4.1 The objection proves too much

The objection says that nobody enacts psychological continuity as a succession rule, so nothing in the account explains its authority. Apply this demand to ordinary promising. Yesterday I promised to help you move on Saturday. Today I would rather not. What gives yesterday's act authority over today's agent?

I have not accepted the obligation anew today. No external authority imposed it. If the reply is "you are the same person, and persons can bind themselves," the objector has accepted that the obligation's reach is set by a relation between stages, namely identity, which was not enacted and whose authority is not separately explained. A double standard results. The objector requires the office view to justify the authority of every non-default rule. Yet the objector treats the default as authoritative merely because it holds.

The objector might answer that identity needs no explanation, because when I bind myself there is no "another" whose agency I commandeer. That answer works only if identity is a deep further fact, something that makes yesterday's agent and today's one in a way that differs in kind from any relation that might hold between me and someone else. Parfit's reductionism challenges exactly this. If personal identity just consists in psychological connectedness and continuity, which are relations of degree, then "is the later stage the same person?" may be indeterminate, and when it is determinate it adds nothing beyond the continuity relations (Parfit 1984, chs. 10–15). Parfit saw that this endangers the authority of commitments over time. His Russian nobleman, who asks his wife to hold him to promises his later and more conservative self will want to abandon, puts the question of whether an earlier self can bind a later one that has changed sharply (Parfit 1984, §106). On reductionism, the dilemma's "own promise versus promise for another" contrast becomes a continuum, and the objector owes us an account of where on it, and why, the authority of self-binding gives out.

My claim is conditional; I do not assert reductionism. The objection needs a non-reductionist premise it does not state, because only the deep-further-fact view lets "identity" ground authority without explanation. Without that premise, ordinary intrapersonal promising raises the same question of authority the objection raises against the office view. It then needs the same kind of answer, which I give in §5. On either option the office view is no worse off than the standard view.

### 4.2 Defaults are not enacted, and need not be

The demand that defaults be enacted also misreads how normative practices operate. In many jurisdictions the law provides intestacy rules. Nobody enacts them for a particular decedent, and they hold *unless* displaced by a will. Hart's rule of recognition is not enacted either. It exists as a practice of officials, and its authority is not a further enactment (Hart 1961, ch. VI). Promising is a normative practice with its own defaults. Conventionalists from Hume to Rawls (Hume 1739–40, III.ii.5; Rawls 1955) hold that the practice's rules *are* its defaults. On Scanlon's expectation-based account, the content of a promise is set by what the promisee is reasonably led to expect (Scanlon 1998, ch. 7). On either account, a default succession rule is the rule that the practice's point selects when the parties have specified nothing else.

Psychological continuity is the default for a clear reason. In ordinary promising, the promisee relies on *the promisor's own future agency*: the promisor's future deliberation, guided by memory of the commitment and by the reasons it supplies. That reliance can be met only by an agent who will be able to recall and act on the promise. So the practice, by default, follows the promise along the chain of psychological continuity, and the default has the practice's authority. Nothing extra is needed.

### 4.3 Continuity cannot be the whole default

There is a further reason to treat psychological continuity as a *rule* rather than a fact that settles itself. On its own it does not yield a determinate obligor.

Consider an AI assistant. On Monday it tells a user, "I won't share what you've told me with anyone, and I'll check back on this tomorrow." By Tuesday the deployment runs forty concurrent instances, each loaded with the same weights and given access to Monday's conversation log. Each is psychologically continuous with Monday's instance, in whatever sense such systems allow, to the same degree. Is the user owed forty check-ins? Has the promise been kept if one instance checks in and thirty-nine do not? If one instance shares the information, are the other thirty-nine in breach?

The natural answers are one check-in, kept, and one breach attributable to "the assistant." Continuity cannot produce these answers, because continuity is one–many and branches. Parfit's response to fission was to make identity depend on a non-branching clause. That clause is extrinsic: whether I am identical to a later person depends on whether some third thing exists. It is hard to see how such a fact could matter to promissory obligation. The promisee's claim should not depend on whether a duplicate happens to exist in another data center. What gives the right answers is an *office*: "the assistant on this user's account." It has one seat with many possible executors, and any one of them can discharge or breach on the seat's behalf.

The point carries back to natural persons. Branching does not occur there, so the default can attach to continuity without trouble. The default for natural persons is therefore *continuity under a non-branching assumption that the practice supplies*. That is a structural rule, not a natural fact. The office is the basic notion, and continuity is how the practice usually fills it in.

### 4.4 A complication confirms the view

The default for natural persons is not purely psychological, and this supports my view. Someone who suffers total retrograde amnesia still owes her debts. Her mortgage does not lapse, and her pledge to a charity is still a claim on her income. She is a determinate seat, fixed by bodily and biographical continuity, legal personality and the network of holdings and relationships that make up her place in social life. What psychological discontinuity does undermine is her role as *executor* of promises whose content needs the original agency. A promise to keep a friend's secret cannot be kept by someone who no longer knows the secret. A promise to "be there for you as I've always been" cannot be performed by someone with no shared history to draw on.

So the default succession rule for the office of being this person has two layers. The seat succeeds by broad continuity of the life, meaning body, legal standing and holdings. Executorship of agency-engaging content succeeds by psychological continuity. In almost every case the two layers coincide, so identity looks like the single condition that does all the work. The office view explains both why it looks that way and when the two layers come apart.

## 5. The authority of non-default rules

### 5.1 The question is not begged

Now the central part of the objection. If the earlier stage sets the succession rule, then whether it has the authority to do so is exactly what is in question. I agree that it is in question. I deny that the office view assumes the answer. The view answers it in the way any normative power is justified.

Raz's account is the standard one. A normative power exists when there is reason to regard acts of a certain kind as changing normative situations, and that reason lies in the value of agents being able to change them in that way (Raz 1972). Owens adds that promising serves the promisee's "authority interest," an interest in having the say over whether the promisor performs, and the promisor's interest in being able to give that authority (Owens 2012, chs. 6–7). So the question is not whether the earlier stage simply *has* authority over the later stage. The question is whether there is sufficient value in agents being able to issue promises whose seat follows a specified succession rule beyond the default.

Plainly there is. Take a woman diagnosed with early-onset Alzheimer's. She wants to assure her adult daughter: "When I can no longer manage, I will go into residential care. I won't ask you to give up your career to nurse me, and I'm putting that in place now." The assurance lets the daughter plan her life, spares her guilt, and expresses the mother's care. It is worth something only if it *binds*, which means it gives the daughter a claim she can rely on and only she can waive. The mother's interest in being able to give it is among the strongest interests anyone has in normative powers. It concerns the last part of her life and her most important relationships. If the power to bind the seat beyond the end of continuity did not exist, she could not give this assurance. She could only make a prediction or express a wish, and her daughter would know the difference. On Raz's own criterion, then, the power is justified.

Note what this argument does *not* claim. It does not claim the earlier stage has authority because it is identical to the later one, which would beg the question. It does not claim authority because the later stage consented. It claims that the power exists because of the interests it serves. Every normative power, including ordinary promising, is justified this way.

### 5.2 Independent evidence: powers that already outrun continuity

The power I attribute is not new. Practice already recognizes, without controversy, normative powers whose reach goes past the end of psychological continuity.

- **Wills and promises to bequeath.** A promise to leave someone property binds the estate after death, and in many legal systems contracts to make a will are enforceable against it.
- **Advance directives.** Competent people may issue instructions that govern their treatment once incapacitated. Whether such directives override the later patient's current interests is disputed: Dworkin (1993) defends their authority by appeal to "precedent autonomy," and Dresser (1986) and others resist. But nearly everyone grants that they have *some* authority over the incapacitated person's care.
- **Lasting powers of attorney.** One can designate who will exercise one's powers once one cannot.
- **Instructions about the body after death,** such as organ donation and burial wishes.

Each of these is a power exercised by an earlier stage, setting a position-specific succession rule or standing instruction that governs a later situation in which the earlier stage's psychological life is absent. The office view puts promising alongside these powers. The objector who denies that the earlier stage can set succession rules for promises must either deny all of them or explain why promising is the exception. Promising is if anything the *least* likely exception, since it is the paradigm of a power to bind one's future.

### 5.3 The limits of the power, and why they meet the consent principle

The principle that one cannot promise on another's behalf without authority is sound. A justified power has limits, and four of them follow from the same interests that justify this one. Together they explain *why* the consent principle holds, and show that the office view respects it.

**(i) Publicity.** The succession rule must be specifiable *at the time of promising* in a way the promisee can know about. The promisee's reliance is what gives the promise its point. A rule nobody could have known about cannot shape what she relies on, and so cannot extend the promise's reach. This is the rationale for "publicly specifiable succession rules" in the original thesis. It also means a later declaration by the promisor cannot add a successor the promisee never relied on.

**(ii) Domain.** The power binds the *seat*, and the seat's domain is whatever the office actually holds: its property, its standing arrangements, its body (within limits I come to), and its relations with third parties who have agreed to be bound to it. The earlier stage cannot extend the seat's domain by fiat to cover what belongs to someone else.

**(iii) Non-delegability of agency-engaging content.** Some promised performances can only be carried out by a particular agency: "I will forgive you," "I will keep your confidence," "I'll come to your recitals myself." Once the agent able to perform these is gone, they lapse, because they cannot be passed on. Contract law recognizes the same point when it discharges personal-service contracts on death or incapacity. The earlier stage may set a succession rule for the seat. It cannot make another agent's attitudes or personal performance the content of its own promise.

**(iv) Standing of successors.** If a later occupant of the seat is an agent with standing of its own, meaning a capacity to value, deliberate and take up commitments, then binding the seat does not override that standing. It enters the deliberation of executors as a promissory claim to be weighed against the occupant's present standing. It does not license coercion.

The consent principle follows directly from these limits. Promising "on another's behalf" is objectionable because it tries to dispose of *another's* domain or *another's* agency. Limits (ii) and (iii) forbid both. What the principle does not forbid, and could not forbid without also forbidding wills, advance directives and corporate continuity, is binding a seat whose domain was the promisor's own when the promise was made. The later stage does not come to that seat bringing an earlier claim of its own on that domain. It *receives* the domain as successor, along with the seat's existing encumbrances, as an heir receives an estate subject to its debts. The question "by what authority does *E* bind *L*?" assumes *L* has its own territory into which *E* is intruding. Within the seat's domain, *L*'s holding comes after *E*'s and is subject to it. Where *L* does have its own territory, namely its present agency and standing, limits (iii) and (iv) protect it.

### 5.4 The other horn: third-party rules

The objection's other horn says that if carers, deployers or the law set the rule, the result is imposed role-obligation. Some third-party rules are like that. A hospital policy requiring nurses to honor patients' informal promises would be a role-obligation, and it fails the release test because the hospital can rescind it.

Many third-party rules play a different part, however. They *constitute the office within which the promise is made*. A corporate charter, granted under law, does not impose obligations on anyone's promise. It creates a position from which promises can be made with determinate reach. The legal order's authority to create such positions is presupposed, but what it authorizes is the existence of a seat, not the content of any particular obligation. Once the position exists and someone promises from it, the release test applies and the obligation is promissory. Likewise, a deployer who publicly specifies that "commitments made by the assistant on your account persist across sessions and model updates" does not *impose* the AI's promises. The deployer makes it possible for the assistant to make promises that reach that far. Whether a particular promise is made, and what it says, is up to the promissory act. The third party supplies the frame and the promise supplies the obligation. Running these two contributions together is what makes the objection's second horn look decisive.

## 6. The three cases revisited

**Corporate promising.** The corporation is the seat and its officers are executors. Succession rules are explicit (charter, corporate law, merger and successor-liability doctrine), and the promisee knows them or could. Verdict: binding across total turnover. It *feels* unproblematic because every element is public and the seat has no agential standing of its own to compete with the claim.

**Severe dementia.** Take the mother from §5.1. She promised her daughter, while competent, that she would enter residential care. She set up a standing instruction and named a proxy. Later she has no psychological continuity with her earlier self, and she resists the move. The seat persists through the person-office's default for seats (her life, holdings and legal standing), supplemented by the rule she set out publicly. The promise binds the seat. The daughter holds a claim only she can waive, and the proxy, an executor by acceptance, owes it to *the daughter* to arrange the move. This is not a duty of the proxy's own design.

Limit (iv) applies in full, though. If the later woman retains the capacity to value, which Jaworska (1999) argues persists far into Alzheimer's disease, then her current values have standing. They must be weighed against the promissory claim. They do not cancel it automatically, and the claim does not override them automatically. The office view therefore takes a position between Dworkin and Dresser that neither occupies. Precedent autonomy governs the *seat*, and Dresser's concern with the present patient is honored through the *standing* limit on execution. The case feels fraught because two sources of standing really do meet within one office. The office view finds the conflict. It does not settle it in advance.

If she had set up nothing, the verdict depends on content. Seat-binding commitments such as "my savings will go to your children's education" survive by the default for seats. Agency-engaging commitments such as "I'll always tell you the truth about how I'm feeling" lapse with continuity, because no succession rule passes them on and they cannot be delegated.

**The redeployed AI.** The verdict depends on whether a public succession rule exists. If the deployer has specified that commitments persist on the account, the office "assistant on this account" is the seat. Any instance serving the account is a default executor, and the branching problem of §4.3 is resolved. If no such rule exists, nothing selects the redeployed system as successor. Continuity alone cannot, since it branches, and the user had nothing determinate to rely on. The promise lapses. This verdict explains the unease many users feel about AI "promises": that unease does not come from doubt about whether the system means what it says. It comes from the lack of a public rule saying *who* will be held to it. The case feels uncertain because the succession rules for this office are still being written.

One question remains open: whether a later AI system with real agential standing could be bound as an *executor* without accepting the role. Limit (iv) says it can be bound as seat-occupant, which means its deployment's resources and arrangements are encumbered. Its own agency, if it has some, is engaged only to the extent that it was constituted to take up the office's commitments, or accepts them. This is no special weakness of the view. It mirrors the natural-person case, in which nobody "accepts" being the continuation of themselves, and the default nonetheless binds.

## 7. Residual worries

*"You have weakened the thesis. It now claims only that seats are bound, not later stages."* No. On my account being bound has always meant occupying a seat, and, where one can act and the content permits, executing. In the ordinary case the two coincide. When we say a demented person is not "bound" by her promises, we usually mean she is not an executor, which my view grants. It does not follow that her promises place no claims on her life. The thesis as originally stated said the later stage is bound *only if* an office with succession rules selects it. This section confirms that and says what the binding consists in.

*"The office of 'being this person' is just personal identity under another name."* It is not. It differs in extension (§4.4: the amnesiac remains a seat), in structure (§4.3: it supplies a non-branching rule that continuity cannot), and in modal profile (§5: it can be extended by public rules within limits). A notion that diverges from identity in all three ways is not identity renamed.

*"Doesn't the power to fix succession rules let people bind distant, unknowing successors?"* Limits (i)–(iv) prevent this. A rule nobody could rely on does not extend the promise. Another's domain cannot be encumbered. Non-delegable content lapses. Successors with standing are not conscripted. What remains is no more than a careful person can already do with a will, a trust and an advance directive. My contribution is to see that promising was always part of the same family of powers.

*"Isn't office-occupancy itself grounded in acceptance, so that acceptance is the true source after all?"* For executors, often yes. For seats, no. Nobody accepts being the seat of their own life, and estates come into being without anyone's acceptance. Some seats are created by acceptance, as when one becomes a trustee. Even then, what the promisee can claim is fixed by the promise made from the seat. It is not fixed by the terms on which the occupant accepted the seat. When the earlier trustee promised and a new one took over, the new trustee's acceptance determines *whether she executes*. It does not determine *what is owed*.

## 8. Conclusion

The referee's objection correctly finds that, in many office cases, the duties of the agents who carry out inherited commitments come from acceptance or role and not from the original promise. Its mistake is to infer that the promise itself has stopped binding. Separating seats from executors shows that executor duties are means of discharge. The obligation they discharge stays promissory by every test of source: release, content, direction and persistence. The objection's demand that succession rules have independent authority applies equally to the default it treats as unproblematic. Answered properly, it shows that the earlier stage's power to fix succession rules is an ordinary normative power. Its justification is the same as any other's, its limits are what make the principle against promising on another's behalf correct, and existing practice already recognizes it in wills, directives and estates.

Personal identity matters to promising because, in the office of being this person, the practice uses psychological continuity to decide who must act and continuity of the life to decide what is encumbered. Where these give out and nothing public takes their place, the promise lapses. Where something public does take their place, the promise continues as the same promise, made by the same obligor in the sense that matters, though not by the same person.
